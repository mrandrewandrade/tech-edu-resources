Engineering Preparation Track

Optional mathematical and engineering enrichment connected to TEJ

A concise map of optional engineering preparation in mathematics, modelling, energy, programming, measurement, and control.
NoteWork in progress

This optional track adds engineering mathematics and modelling to regular Computer Engineering Technology work. It is locally developed, is not endorsed by a college or university, and does not guarantee admission or postsecondary credit. Some assignments and the thermal-process project still require classroom testing.

Use this page as a pathway map. The TEJ sidebar contains the available lessons, worksheets, labs, projects, and reference notes. Detailed implementation planning belongs in the teacher guide.

The chapter names use terminology found in current University of Waterloo engineering programs, including Chemical Engineering and Mechatronics Engineering.

0. Engineering design and communication

Learning goal: Use an engineering notebook to record requirements, assumptions, diagrams, equations, units, test results, and revisions.

Why it matters: Clear records make work easier to check, improve, and explain.

Subsections:

  1. Engineering baseline
  2. Engineering notebook
  3. Assumptions and evidence
  4. Technical communication

Coming out of this chapter, students can: document a technical problem and show a complete, checkable solution process.

Suggested work: Engineering baseline diagnostic, correction record, learning targets, and notebook setup.

1. Units, engineering notation, and estimation

Learning goal: Convert International System of Units quantities and metric prefixes; use engineering notation, significant figures, dimensional analysis, ratios, rates, and estimation.

Why it matters: Good units and estimates help catch calculator mistakes.

Subsections:

  1. Units and dimensions
  2. Engineering notation
  3. Significant figures
  4. Ratios and rates
  5. Estimation

Coming out of this chapter, students can: convert, estimate, calculate, and communicate technical quantities reliably.

Related TEJ resources: Number Systems, Measurement, Units, and Calculator Skills

Suggested work: Assignment 1: quantity and unit audit.

References: [1, Ch. 0] [27, Ch. 1] [28, Chs. 1, 3]

2. Ohm’s law and electrical power

Learning goal: Calculate voltage, current, resistance, and power using Ohm’s law and the power equations; rearrange equations and check component limits.

Why it matters: These calculations predict whether a circuit will work safely.

Subsections:

  1. Charge
  2. Voltage
  3. Current
  4. Resistance
  5. Ohm’s law
  6. Electrical power
  7. Operating limits

Coming out of this chapter, students can: derive and check a resistive design rather than only substitute into a formula.

Related TEJ resources: Basic Electronics

Suggested work: Complete the Basic Circuit Calculations worksheet, then prepare Assignment 2: resistive design brief.

Reference: [1, Chs. 1, 3]

3. Series, parallel, and series-parallel circuits

Learning goal: Calculate equivalent resistance, source current, branch current, voltage drops, and power in series, parallel, and series-parallel resistor circuits.

Why it matters: Students can predict circuit behaviour before building and find faults afterward.

Subsections:

  1. Series resistance and voltage drops
  2. Parallel resistance and branch currents
  3. Series-parallel reduction
  4. Current and voltage checks
  5. Power checks

Coming out of this chapter, students can: solve a network and prove the solution with voltage, current, resistance, and power checks.

Suggested work: Assignment 3: series-parallel design proof.

Reference: [1, Chs. 4–6]

4. Kirchhoff’s laws and systems of equations

Learning goal: Apply Kirchhoff’s current law at nodes and Kirchhoff’s voltage law around loops; write, solve, and check two- and three-variable linear systems.

Why it matters: Many real circuits cannot be solved with one equation.

Subsections:

  1. Systems of linear equations
  2. Kirchhoff’s current law
  3. Kirchhoff’s voltage law
  4. Matrices
  5. Residual and power checks

Coming out of this chapter, students can: translate a physical network into simultaneous equations and verify the solution by hand and with software.

Suggested work: Assignment 4: branch-current system solved by hand and checked with software.

References: [1, Chs. 7, 9] [28, Chs. 9, 12]

5. Equivalent circuits and optimization

Learning goal: Use voltage and current dividers, Thévenin and Norton equivalent circuits, source and load models, maximum power transfer, constraints, and sensitivity.

Why it matters: A simpler model makes a complex design easier to compare and improve.

Subsections:

  1. Voltage and current dividers
  2. Thévenin and Norton equivalents
  3. Source and load models
  4. Maximum power transfer
  5. Constraints
  6. Sensitivity

Coming out of this chapter, students can: recommend a design and defend it using a model, constraints, evidence, and trade-offs.

Suggested work: Assignment 5: source and load optimization memo.

References: [1, Ch. 10] [28, Chs. 13–16]

6. Material and energy balances

Learning goal: Draw a system boundary and calculate material or energy input, output, accumulation, loss, and efficiency.

Why it matters: A balance shows where material or energy goes and what is being lost.

Subsections:

  1. System boundaries
  2. Process variables
  3. Material balances
  4. Energy balances
  5. Accumulation
  6. Efficiency

Coming out of this chapter, students can: complete an energy audit or supervised low-voltage thermal investigation and explain the losses.

Related TEJ resources: Power and Energy and Computer Power and Parts

Suggested work: Assignment 6: energy audit; Lab 1: supervised low-voltage thermal balance.

References: [1, Chs. 1, 12] [27, Chs. 3, 5–6]

ImportantSafety boundary

Use only teacher-approved protected low-voltage equipment. Students do not construct or probe mains circuits. Isolate liquids from exposed electronics and define temperature, current, containment, and shutdown limits before testing.

7. Resistor-capacitor and thermal transients

Learning goal: Model resistor-capacitor charging and discharging or heating and cooling using transient response, time constants, and numerical stepping.

Why it matters: Real systems do not change instantly. Time constants also explain how circuits smooth noisy signals and why temperature sensors respond gradually.

Subsections:

  1. Transient response
  2. Resistor-capacitor charging and discharging
  3. Time constants
  4. Signal smoothing and filtering
  5. Thermal response and sensor lag
  6. Numerical stepping and model stability

Coming out of this chapter, students can: model a changing electrical or thermal system and explain how a resistor-capacitor network can smooth a sensor signal.

Suggested work: Assignment 7: resistor-capacitor or thermal transient model.

References: [1, Chs. 16, 18–22] [28, Ch. 25]

8. Sensors, measurement, calibration, and uncertainty

Learning goal: Select and calibrate sensors; collect repeatable data; report range, sensitivity, resolution, accuracy, repeatability, error, and uncertainty.

Why it matters: Measurements are useful only when students know how much to trust them.

Subsections:

  1. Sensor selection and operating range
  2. Signal conditioning and data acquisition
  3. Calibration
  4. Sensitivity and resolution
  5. Accuracy and repeatability
  6. Error and uncertainty

Coming out of this chapter, students can: calibrate a sensor, graph its response, quantify error, and state where the model is valid.

Instrumentation and data branch: This chapter and the next connect physical measurements to curve fitting, regression, and machine learning.

Suggested work: Lab 2: sensor calibration and model check.

References: [1, Ch. 8] [28, Chs. 3–4]

9. Curve fitting, regression, and machine learning

Learning goal: Fit and compare models using sensor data, least-squares curve fitting, linear regression, residuals, training and test data, baselines, prediction error, and simple classification.

Why it matters: A model can find useful patterns in data, but it must be tested before it is trusted.

Subsections:

  1. Cleaning and plotting sensor data
  2. Curve fitting and least squares
  3. Linear regression and residuals
  4. Training, validation, and test data
  5. Baselines and model evaluation
  6. Introductory classification
  7. Overfitting, bias, privacy, and limits

Coming out of this chapter, students can: fit a model to non-personal sensor data, measure its error, compare it with a baseline, and explain its limits.

Related TEJ resources: Data Science and Machine Learning

Suggested work: Sensor-data modelling investigation using at least two candidate models and a held-out test set.

References: [28, Chs. 17, 20] [4]

10. Motors and electromechanical systems

Learning goal: Calculate force, torque, rotational speed, electrical input power, mechanical output power, efficiency, and operating limits.

Why it matters: These calculations show how electrical energy produces useful motion.

Subsections:

  1. Force and motion
  2. Torque
  3. Rotational speed
  4. Electrical power
  5. Mechanical power
  6. Efficiency

Coming out of this chapter, students can: characterize an actuator and explain how load affects current, speed, temperature, and efficiency.

Suggested work: Lab 3: actuator characterization.

Reference: [1, Chs. 13–14]

11. Boolean logic, algorithms, and state machines

Learning goal: Use Boolean operators, logic gates, truth tables, algorithms, conditions, programs, state diagrams, and test cases.

Why it matters: These tools turn rules and decisions into systems that can be tested.

Subsections:

  1. Boolean logic
  2. Truth tables
  3. Algorithms
  4. Data structures
  5. State machines
  6. Testing

Coming out of this chapter, students can: create a calculation program or safe state-machine design with documented tests.

Computer-engineering branch: Chapters 11–13 connect logic states, physical outputs, and digital number representation.

Related TEJ resources: Digital Logic and Stable Inputs

Suggested work: Assignment 8: engineering calculation program; Assignment 9: safety state machine; Digital Inputs worksheet.

Reference: [28, Ch. 2]

12. Sensor, logic, and actuator project

Learning goal: Combine sensor inputs, thresholds, Boolean expressions, truth tables, or state rules to control a motor, fan, relay, alarm, or indicator through a suitable driver circuit.

Why it matters: This project joins sensing, decision making, and physical action in one working system.

Subsections:

  1. Input requirements and sensor thresholds
  2. Stable inputs and signal conditioning
  3. Truth table and Boolean expression
  4. Output driver and flyback protection
  5. Motor, relay, alarm, or indicator output
  6. Safe states and interlocks
  7. Test cases and fault handling

Coming out of this chapter, students can: build or simulate a system that reads sensor states, applies documented logic, and safely controls a physical output.

Related TEJ resources: Digital Logic and Stable Inputs and Control Systems and Computer Numerical Control

Suggested work: Project: sensor-controlled motor, fan, relay, alarm, or indicator with a truth table, schematic, safe output driver, tests, and short explanation.

Safety: Use teacher-approved low-voltage equipment, motor or relay drivers, current limiting, and flyback protection where required.

References: [1, Chs. 13–14] [28, Ch. 2]

13. Number systems and digital representation

Learning goal: Represent values in binary, decimal, and hexadecimal; interpret bits, bytes, words, Boolean values, signed and unsigned integers, two’s complement, fixed-point and floating-point numbers, range, resolution, and overflow.

Why it matters: A computer can only store a limited pattern of bits, so the representation determines which values it can use and what can go wrong.

Subsections:

  1. Binary, decimal, and hexadecimal
  2. Bits, bytes, and words
  3. Boolean values
  4. Unsigned integers
  5. Signed integers and two’s complement
  6. Fixed-point and floating-point representation
  7. Range, resolution, quantization, and overflow

Coming out of this chapter, students can: convert common number representations, choose a suitable data type, and predict range, resolution, and overflow problems.

Related TEJ resources: Number Systems and Digital Logic and Stable Inputs

Suggested work: Digital representation worksheet using sensor readings, integer ranges, hexadecimal values, and overflow cases.

Reference: [1]

14. Feedback and control systems

Learning goal: Compare open-loop, on-off, hysteresis, proportional, integral, and derivative control using rise time, overshoot, steady-state error, stability, and saturation.

Why it matters: Feedback helps a system correct itself, but poor settings can make it slow or unstable.

Subsections:

  1. Open-loop control
  2. Feedback
  3. Process control
  4. Response
  5. Stability
  6. Saturation
  7. Safety

Coming out of this chapter, students can: compare control methods using common graphs, response measures, and safety criteria.

Related TEJ resources: Control Systems and Computer Numerical Control

Suggested work: Assignment 10: control-method comparison using the Control Methods Comparison handout.

References: [27, Ch. 3] [28, Chs. 16, 20]

15. Engineering design project

Learning goal: Write requirements; create a model, schematic, and program; select sensors and actuators; build or simulate; test, validate, and revise.

Why it matters: The project shows that students can combine the course skills into one working system.

Subsections:

  1. Requirements
  2. Engineering design
  3. Prototype
  4. Experiment
  5. Validation
  6. Optimization
  7. Technical communication

Coming out of this chapter, students can: deliver an instrumented system supported by a mathematical model, test evidence, revision, and a concise technical report.

Suggested project: Instrumented low-voltage thermal process controller. Alternatives may use a motor or pump test stand, battery and thermal monitor, controlled fan, tank-level simulator, or renewable-energy demonstrator.

Deliverable: Requirements, model, schematic, build or approved simulation, code, validation, revision, and concise technical report.

Core references

The textbooks are teacher references. Public activities use original explanations, diagrams, values, and questions.

[4] National Institute of Standards and Technology, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework supports responsible model evaluation, risk analysis, transparency, and human oversight.

[28] Chapra and Canale, Numerical Methods for Engineers, 6th edition supports modelling, error, linear systems, optimization, curve fitting, programming, and numerical time-step models.
