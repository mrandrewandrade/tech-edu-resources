Engineering Preparation Track

Curriculum implementation plan

A progressive engineering curriculum built around circuits, mathematics, modelling, measurement, programming, and control.
NoteWork in progress

This is a curriculum implementation plan. Completed lessons are linked as they become classroom-ready; later sections show the intended learning sequence.

The track extends regular TEJ work with mathematics and modelling used in first-year engineering. It is useful for students interested in electrical, computer, mechatronics, mechanical, systems, or chemical engineering.

Engineering calculations

I will be able to use units, metric prefixes, engineering notation, significant figures, ratios, estimation, and the GUESS method.

This is useful because I can catch mistakes and communicate a calculation clearly.

Used in college or university: Every engineering course expects correct units, notation, and documented calculations. [1, Chs. 0–1]

Ohm’s law

I will be able to calculate voltage, current, and resistance and check whether the result is physically reasonable.

This is useful because I can predict the basic behaviour of a circuit before I build it.

Used in college or university: Circuit analysis, electronics laboratories, and design courses. [1, Chs. 1, 3]

Series circuits

I will be able to calculate total resistance, circuit current, and voltage drops in a one-path circuit.

This is useful because I can predict and check every value in a complete series circuit.

Used in college or university: Direct-current circuit analysis and introductory electrical laboratories. [1, Ch. 4]

Open the Series Circuits lesson package

Parallel circuits

I will be able to calculate equivalent resistance, source current, and branch currents in a parallel circuit.

This is useful because I can explain how several loads operate from the same source voltage.

Used in college or university: Network analysis, electrical distribution, instrumentation, and electronics. [1, Ch. 5]

Series-parallel circuits

I will be able to identify series and parallel groups, redraw a circuit, reduce it step by step, and work back to each branch value.

This is useful because I can turn a mixed circuit into smaller problems and prove the final answer.

Used in college or university: Network reduction, circuit laboratories, and preparation for Kirchhoff analysis. [1, Ch. 6]

Open the Series-Parallel Circuits lesson package

Kirchhoff’s laws

I will be able to apply Kirchhoff’s current law at a node and Kirchhoff’s voltage law around a loop.

This is useful because I can write equations for circuits that cannot be reduced by inspection.

Used in college or university: Nodal analysis, mesh analysis, electronics, power systems, and control hardware. [1, Ch. 9]

TipRecommended Grob sequence

Chapter 9 can follow Chapter 6. Students already have the series, parallel, and reduction skills needed for Kirchhoff analysis. Teach the relevant meter-safety material from Chapter 8 before physical measurements; Chapter 7 can be returned to later.

Linear algebra for circuits

I will be able to turn node and loop equations into a two- or three-variable system and solve it by elimination, substitution, matrices, and software.

This is useful because I can solve several unknown currents or voltages at the same time.

Used in college or university: Linear algebra, numerical methods, statics, structures, circuits, process models, and control. [28, Chs. 9, 12]

Work, energy, power, and efficiency

I will be able to distinguish work, energy, and power; use their derived units; and compare electrical energy input with useful mechanical work output.

This is useful because I can estimate battery use, motor performance, operating time, losses, and whether a device is doing useful work efficiently.

Planned laboratory: A small motor lifts a known mass through a measured height. Students measure motor voltage, current, and lift time, then compare the electrical energy supplied with the mechanical work completed and calculate input power, output power, and efficiency.

Used in college or university: Physics, mechanics, thermodynamics, circuits, electromechanical systems, and preparation for energy balances. [1, Chs. 3, 12, 14]

Equivalent circuits and optimization

I will be able to use dividers, Thévenin and Norton equivalents, maximum power transfer, constraints, and sensitivity.

This is useful because I can replace a complicated source network with a simpler model and compare designs.

Used in college or university: Circuit design, modelling, optimization, and instrumentation. [1, Chs. 7, 10]

Material and energy balances

I will be able to define a system boundary and calculate input, output, accumulation, loss, and efficiency.

This is useful because I can show where material or energy goes and explain what is lost.

Used in college or university: Chemical, mechanical, environmental, and energy engineering. [27, Chs. 3, 5–6]

Transient systems

I will be able to model resistor-capacitor charging, signal smoothing, and heating or cooling with time constants and numerical steps.

This is useful because I can predict how a real system changes over time instead of assuming it responds instantly.

Used in college or university: Differential equations, circuits, heat transfer, process dynamics, and control. [1, Ch. 22]

Boolean logic, algorithms, and state machines

I will be able to interpret switches and digital sensor inputs as binary states and use logic gates, truth tables, Boolean expressions, algorithms, state diagrams, and test cases.

This is useful because I can turn physical on-or-off inputs into clear decisions that can be tested.

Used in college or university: Digital logic, programming, computer architecture, embedded systems, and control.

Number systems and digital representation

I will be able to use binary, decimal, hexadecimal, signed and unsigned integers, two’s complement, fixed-point, floating-point, analog-to-digital converter codes, pulse-width-modulation duty cycles, range, resolution, and overflow.

This is useful because I can explain how a computer stores a switch state, represents a sensor reading, and commands a variable output.

Used in college or university: Programming, computer engineering, embedded systems, numerical methods, and digital signal processing.

Sensors and measurement

I will be able to connect pull-up and pull-down switch inputs, read digital sensors, convert analog sensor signals with an analog-to-digital converter, use pulse-width modulation for controlled outputs, calibrate measurements, and report range, sensitivity, resolution, accuracy, repeatability, error, and uncertainty.

This is useful because I can move from a simple on-or-off input to a measured value and a controlled output while deciding whether the data is trustworthy.

Used in college or university: Engineering laboratories, instrumentation, embedded systems, robotics, experiments, and process control. [1, Ch. 8]

Motors and electromechanical systems

I will be able to calculate force, torque, speed, electrical input power, mechanical output power, efficiency, and operating limits.

This is useful because I can connect circuit behaviour and pulse-width-modulated control to useful motion.

Used in college or university: Mechanical, electrical, mechatronics, robotics, and systems engineering. [1, Chs. 13–14]

Sensor, logic, and actuator project

I will be able to combine digital or analog sensor inputs, thresholds, Boolean logic, pulse-width modulation, and a protected driver circuit to control a motor, fan, relay, alarm, or indicator.

This is useful because I can build a complete system that senses, decides, and acts safely.

Used in college or university: Mechatronics, embedded systems, robotics, automation, and design projects.

Curve fitting, regression, and machine learning

I will be able to fit a model to sensor data, inspect residuals, separate training and test data, and compare prediction error with a baseline.

This is useful because I can find a useful pattern without assuming the first model is correct.

Used in college or university: Data analysis, numerical methods, system identification, experiments, and introductory machine learning. [28, Chs. 17, 20]

Operational amplifiers and calculus

I will be able to analyze ideal operational-amplifier circuits and connect integrator and differentiator circuits to slope, area, and rate of change.

This is useful because I can see calculus operate on a real voltage signal.

Used in college or university: Calculus, analog electronics, instrumentation, signals, and control. [1, Ch. 33]

Feedback and control

I will be able to compare open-loop, on-off, hysteresis, proportional, integral, and derivative control using response, error, stability, and saturation.

This is useful because I can make a system correct itself and explain why poor settings cause problems.

Used in college or university: Control systems, robotics, mechatronics, chemical processes, and automation.

Engineering design project

I will be able to define requirements, model a system, create a schematic and program, select sensors and actuators, build or simulate, test, validate, and revise.

This is useful because I can combine the track into one working, evidence-based design.

Used in college or university: Design courses, laboratories, capstone projects, and technical communication.

Ontario course collaboration opportunities

These are places where teachers can plan shared investigations or projects. They do not mean that one course replaces the learning in another course.

Computer engineering technology

Courses: TEJ3M and TEJ4M

Shared work: Circuit calculations, schematics, simulation, construction, measurement, troubleshooting, sensors, motors, digital logic, data representation, and pulse-width modulation.

Good collaboration: TEJ students can design, build, program, and troubleshoot the measurement or control system used by another class. This connects directly to TEJ3M A3.1–A3.3, B3.1–B3.4, and B5.3, and TEJ4M A3.2–A3.5, A5.2–A5.6, and B3.1–B3.4. Ontario Technological Education curriculum

Physics

Course: SPH3U

Shared work: Charge, voltage, current, resistance, power, energy, series circuits, parallel circuits, mixed circuits, Ohm’s law, Kirchhoff’s laws, meters, motors, and generators.

Good collaboration: Use the same circuit problem set and laboratory data in both courses. Physics can emphasize the model and equations; TEJ can emphasize the schematic, safe construction, measurement, and troubleshooting. This supports SPH3U F2.1–F2.3, F2.6, F2.8, and F3.3–F3.9. SPH3U expectations

Chemistry

Course: SCH4U

Shared work: Electrochemical cells, batteries, cell potential, current, energy, efficiency, data collection, graphing, uncertainty, and significant figures.

Good collaboration: Chemistry students can build a galvanic cell and explain the redox reactions while TEJ students measure its open-circuit voltage, loaded voltage, current, power, and internal resistance. This connects especially to SCH4U F2.4–F2.5 and the A1 scientific-investigation skills. SCH4U expectations

Biology

Course: SBI4U

Shared work: Sensors, calibration, data logging, graphs, uncertainty, stimulus-response investigations, and feedback systems.

Good collaboration: TEJ students can build and validate a light, temperature, moisture, heart-rate, or respiration data logger. Biology students then use it to investigate a biological question and interpret the evidence. This connects naturally to SBI4U A1 and E2.2–E2.4; the electronics are the instrument, not a substitute for the biology. SBI4U expectations

Shared science practices

All three science partnerships can use the same habits taught at the start of this track: choose suitable instruments, control variables, record data in tables and graphs, analyse error and uncertainty, use SI units, and report calculations with appropriate significant figures.

Core references

The textbooks guide the sequence and level. Student materials use original explanations, diagrams, values, and questions.

[1] Grob’s Basic Electronics, 12th edition: direct-current circuits, measurement, network analysis, transients, and operational amplifiers.

[27] Çengel, Introduction to Thermodynamics and Heat Transfer, 2nd edition: units, energy balances, efficiency, and transient thermal behaviour.

[28] Chapra and Canale, Numerical Methods for Engineers, 6th edition: linear systems, modelling, regression, optimization, and numerical methods.
