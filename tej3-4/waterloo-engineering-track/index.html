Waterloo Engineering Track

Optional computer engineering technology enrichment in mathematics, modelling, energy, programming, and control

A progressive engineering-enrichment pathway built around practical course work.
NoteIndependent enrichment pathway

This optional track adds engineering mathematics and modelling to regular Computer Engineering Technology work. It is locally developed, is not endorsed by the University of Waterloo, and does not guarantee admission or university credit.

WarningPlanning status

This is a work-in-progress roadmap. Linked course lessons may be used now. The new assignments, answer keys, and thermal-process project are placeholders until they are developed, checked, and classroom-tested.

Why this track

First-year engineering asks students to turn physical situations into equations, solve them carefully, test their assumptions, and explain the result. This track builds those habits through circuits, measurements, programs, sensors, actuators, energy, and control systems.

The preparation is useful across electrical, computer, mechanical, mechatronics, systems, chemical, civil, environmental, and related programs. Waterloo’s current program pages provide examples for Electrical Engineering, Mechanical Engineering, Mechatronics Engineering, Systems Design Engineering, and Chemical Engineering.

Every activity returns to four questions:

  1. What is the system boundary?
  2. What quantities enter, leave, accumulate, or are transformed?
  3. What equations and assumptions represent the system?
  4. Do calculated, simulated, and measured results agree closely enough to support the conclusion?

Learning goals and why they matter

Learning goal Why it matters
Define variables, units, directions, assumptions, and the system boundary before calculating. A clear model prevents hidden assumptions and sign errors.
Use dimensional analysis, engineering notation, significant figures, estimation, and the Given–Unknown–Equation–Substitute–Solve method. Units and estimates expose impossible calculator results.
Derive equations from circuit and system diagrams. Engineers must model unfamiliar systems, not only recognize familiar formulas.
Solve and verify linear systems with two or three variables. Coupled equations appear in circuits, structures, processes, controls, and data models.
Apply conservation of charge, current, voltage, power, energy, and material. Conservation laws connect many branches of engineering.
Collect repeatable data, calibrate a model, and quantify error and uncertainty. Measurements are evidence only when their limits are understood.
Build a step-by-step model in a spreadsheet or program and compare it with measurements. Computational models let engineers predict changing systems and test ideas safely.
Compare designs using constraints, efficiency, sensitivity, cost, safety, and uncertainty. Engineering decisions require trade-offs rather than one perfect answer.
Design, build, program, test, revise, and explain an instrumented system. A complete project joins mathematical reasoning with practical work and communication.

Pathway structure

The suggested pathway adds approximately 35 to 50 hours across a full course. Most work replaces simpler practice or adds one challenge section. Chapters 1 through 6 form the analytical core; later chapters can match the student’s interests and available equipment.

Chapter specifications

Chapter 0: Engineering baseline and notebook

The track begins with a short diagnostic rather than a mark. The student demonstrates fraction and decimal operations, equation rearrangement, graph interpretation, unit conversions, calculator use, estimation, and a short technical explanation. The result determines which skills require review.

The engineering notebook standard requires a dated problem statement, system diagram, givens, unknowns, assumptions, equations before substitution, units carried through the work, full calculator result, rounded answer, reasonableness check, and revision record. Safety decisions and failed tests remain part of the record.

Chapter 1: Quantities, units, and scale

Complete Number Systems, Measurement, Units, and Calculator Skills, then extend the work to compound units such as coulombs per second, joules per second, watt-hours, kilograms per second, litres per minute, joules per kilogram-degree Celsius, revolutions per minute, radians per second, and dollars per kilowatt-hour.

The student should convert units by multiplying by identities, not by moving decimals without explanation. Estimation precedes calculator work. Final answers must state whether the magnitude is physically plausible.

Chapter 2: Algebraic electrical models

Complete Charge, Voltage, and Current, Closed, Open, and Short Circuits, and Ohm’s Law.

Track problems require the student to derive and rearrange relationships, identify impossible values, state operating limits, and compare symbolic predictions with simulation. Problems should include design choices rather than only asking for a missing number.

Chapter 3: Conservation in series and parallel networks

Use the regular series and parallel circuit sequence. The extension emphasizes why the current, voltage, resistance, and power rules follow from circuit structure. Each solution includes at least two independent checks.

The student should be able to demonstrate:

\[I_T=I_1=I_2 \quad \text{for one series path}\]

\[V_T=V_1=V_2 \quad \text{for parallel branches across the same nodes}\]

\[P_T=\sum P_k\]

The power equation is used as a conservation check after the circuit has been solved.

Chapter 4: Coupled equations and Kirchhoff’s laws

This is the central systems-of-equations chapter. It progresses from Kirchhoff’s current law and Kirchhoff’s voltage law to branch-current, node-voltage, and mesh-current methods. The student solves a two-source network by elimination, writes the equations in matrix form, and uses software to verify the result. [1, Ch. 9]

The solution includes a labelled schematic, assumed directions, independent node and loop equations, the algebraic solution, substitution into the original equations, a current-law residual, a voltage-law residual, and a power balance. A negative current means the actual direction is opposite to the assumed direction; it does not mean the calculation failed.

Chapter 5: Equivalent models and optimization

Thevenin and Norton equivalents reduce a network to a useful model at two terminals. The student varies a load, graphs delivered power, identifies a maximum, and then explains why maximum power is not always the same design objective as maximum efficiency, minimum cost, lowest temperature, or longest battery life.

This chapter introduces engineering optimization without requiring calculus. A valid recommendation must name the objective, constraints, variables, feasible alternatives, evidence, and trade-offs.

Chapter 6: Power and energy balances

Complete Power and Energy, then apply a conserved-quantity balance:

\[\text{input}-\text{output}=\text{accumulation}\]

For a material species that can be produced or consumed, the more general form also includes generation and consumption terms. For total energy, energy may cross the boundary or change form, but it is conserved.

For an electrical load:

\[P=VI \qquad E_{\mathrm{in}}=VIt\]

For a supervised low-voltage water-heating investigation:

\[E_{\mathrm{water}}=mc\Delta T\]

\[E_{\mathrm{in}}=E_{\mathrm{stored}}+E_{\mathrm{loss}}\]

\[\eta=\frac{E_{\mathrm{useful}}}{E_{\mathrm{in}}}\times100\%\]

The introductory calculation treats water as a closed batch and calculates an observed loss. The advanced investigation uses two experimental runs to estimate an effective heat capacity \(C_{\mathrm{eff}}\) and a heat-loss coefficient \(K\):

\[\overline V_j\overline I_jt_j=C_{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta T_j+K(T_{\mathrm{avg},j}-T_{\mathrm{room},j})t_j\]

Two runs produce two linear equations. A third run tests the model. The teacher background covers energy analysis, closed systems, and control volumes; the student activity remains original, low-voltage, and appropriate for a high-school laboratory. [27, Chs. 3, 5–6]

ImportantThermal-project safety boundary

Use only teacher-approved protected low-voltage equipment. Students do not construct or probe mains circuits. Keep liquids isolated from exposed electronics, include temperature and current limits, provide secondary containment, and define a safe shutdown state before testing.

Chapter 7: Measurement, calibration, and uncertainty

Compare calculated, simulated, and measured values, then calibrate one sensor or actuator using repeated measurements. The student produces a scatter plot, fitted relationship, residual table, stated valid range, and discussion of uncertainty.

The objective is not to force every data set to be linear. The student must explain whether the model is suitable, where interpolation is defensible, and why extrapolation beyond the measured range may be unsafe.

Chapter 8: Transient and step-by-step systems

Resistor-capacitor and resistor-inductor circuits provide a practical route into changing systems. The student measures or simulates a transient response, identifies a time constant, and compares observations with a model. A spreadsheet or program then advances the model one time step at a time.

For the thermal process, an accessible no-calculus model is:

\[T_{n+1}=T_n+\frac{P_{\mathrm{in}}-K(T_n-T_{\mathrm{room}})}{C_{\mathrm{eff}}}\Delta t\]

The student changes the time step and explains why a numerical model can become inaccurate or unstable. Differential equations may be named as the later university language for the same idea, but they are not required to complete the track.

Chapter 9: Logic, algorithms, and state

Complete the Digital Logic and Stable Inputs sequence and the appropriate Programming and Microcontroller modules. Three-input truth tables must count in binary order from \(000\) to \(111\), with a separate column for each output.

The programming extension implements one previously solved model. Suitable programs include a Given–Unknown–Equation–Substitute–Solve calculator with unit checks, a two- or three-equation solver, a resistor-capacitor circuit simulator, a thermal time-step model, or a calibration tool. The student supplies tests, explains invalid-input handling, and compares program output with one hand calculation.

Chapter 10: Electromechanical systems

Use magnetism, electromagnetism, switching, motors, protection, and motion control to connect electrical input to mechanical output. The minimum mathematical set is:

\[P_{\mathrm{electrical}}=VI\]

\[P_{\mathrm{mechanical}}=\tau\omega\]

\[\eta=\frac{P_{\mathrm{mechanical}}}{P_{\mathrm{electrical}}}\]

The student characterizes one approved actuator or pump across several operating points and explains why speed, torque, flow, current, temperature, and efficiency cannot all be optimized independently.

Chapter 11: Feedback, models, and design decisions

Complete the Control Systems and Computer Numerical Control comparison sequence. Use the same process model and disturbance when comparing open-loop, on-off, hysteresis, proportional, proportional-integral, and proportional-integral-derivative control. Record rise time, overshoot, settling behaviour, steady-state error, saturation, and failure response.

Calculus is not required. The student should understand the operational meaning of accumulated error and rate of change, use graphs accurately, and explain how changing a parameter affects the complete system.

Chapter 12: Integrated engineering project

The recommended project is an instrumented low-voltage thermal process controller. It connects circuits, sensors, microcontrollers, programming, energy, data, safety, and control while providing useful preparation across engineering disciplines.

The student may propose a different project when it preserves the same mathematical and validation requirements. Suitable alternatives include a motor or pump test stand, battery discharge and thermal monitor, environmental chamber model, controlled fan-and-temperature system, tank-level simulator, or instrumented renewable-energy demonstrator.

Assessment model

Track work should be assessed as engineering evidence rather than extra arithmetic. The recommended weighting within each major task is:

Category Weight Evidence
Model formulation 25% boundary, variables, assumptions, diagram and equations
Mathematical execution 20% algebra, units, precision, software use and verification
Design and safety 20% ratings, constraints, protection, feasible choices and fault response
Experiment and validation 20% calibration, repeatability, comparison, uncertainty and revision
Communication 15% organized notebook, graphs, citations, explanation and conclusion

A polished answer with an unjustified model should not receive a high track result. A student who discovers and explains why a model failed may demonstrate stronger engineering judgement than a student whose numbers happen to agree.
