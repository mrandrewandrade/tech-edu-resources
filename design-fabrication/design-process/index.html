N.I.C.E. Design Process

A concise design process for defining needs, investigating, communicating, evaluating, and improving.

Learning goals

By the end of this page:

  • I will be able to move from a real situation to a testable idea.
  • I will be able to use evidence and feedback to decide what to change.

This is important because a good project solves a real need and improves through testing.

Start with the situation, move through N.I.C.E., and repeat the process after evaluation. Each step produces something useful for the next step.

Situation: understand what is happening

Before choosing a solution, describe the real situation.

  • Facts can be observed or supported with evidence.
  • Assumptions may be true, but still need to be checked.
  • Interpretations are what you think the facts mean. They can change when you learn more.

The result is a clear situation statement that does not jump ahead to a product or solution.

Use the N.I.C.E. Needs Worksheet to record the situation, users, facts, assumptions, questions, and restrictions.

N: Needs and Necessities

Turn the situation into requirements.

  • A need describes what the solution should accomplish.
  • A necessity is a requirement that must be met.
  • A nice-to-have would improve the solution but is not essential.
  • Priorities show which requirements matter most.

The result is a clear need statement and a prioritized list of requirements. Describe the function you need, not a product you have already decided to build.

Continue in the N.I.C.E. Needs Worksheet to write the need statement and rank the necessities and nice-to-haves.

I: Investigate and Inquire

Find out what already exists and what the evidence suggests.

  1. What do we need?
  2. What solutions already exist?
  3. What could work for this situation?
  4. What should be improved, combined, simplified, or changed?

Compare each idea with the needs, necessities, and priorities. The result is an evidence-based direction and a reason for choosing it.

Use the N.I.C.E. Inquiry Worksheet to ask questions, compare existing solutions, and decide what to adapt.

C: Create and Communicate

Create a sketch, model, prototype, or other representation that lets someone understand the idea. Explain the situation, the need, and how the idea helps. Then ask specific questions instead of only asking, “Do you like it?”

A short pitch can follow this sequence:

  1. Have you ever experienced this situation?
  2. Have you needed or wanted this improvement?
  3. Our idea is… It helps by…

The result is a clear pitch, useful feedback, and one specific change to consider.

Use the communication worksheets in order:

  1. Communicate Worksheet Online or Pitch and Show Your Idea Print to explain and show the idea.
  2. Feedback Package to collect specific feedback and find patterns.
  3. Plan and Plan Feedback to identify resources, roles, steps, and improvements to the plan.

E: Evaluate

Compare the idea or prototype with the original situation and the prioritized requirements. Check whether earlier assumptions were correct. Look for patterns in testing and feedback rather than reacting to one random comment.

Decide what to keep, change, remove, or test next. The result is a revised solution supported by evidence and reasons.

Use the N.I.C.E. Evaluate Worksheet to connect the feedback to a justified next change.

Repeat the cycle

Return to the needs after evaluation. Ask what the evidence proved, which assumption was wrong, which requirement matters most now, and what the next version should change. Design improves through these repeated, evidence-based decisions.

Use the N.I.C.E. Design Log to keep the worksheets, sketches, feedback, decisions, and later versions together.

Watch and use

  • Engineering design process review: notice how the process moves from a problem to testing.
  • Pitch the idea: notice the sequence problem, idea, show it, ask.
  • Open the complete N.I.C.E. slide deck.
  • Open all N.I.C.E. worksheets, completed examples, and classroom materials.
