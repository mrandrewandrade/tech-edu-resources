Systems Design & Fabrication Track

A practical reference from a real need to a documented, tested, multi-process build

A broad design and fabrication reference covering technical drawings, standards, GD&T, fits, machine design, fastening, wood, metal, welding, machining, CNC, laser cutting, 3D printing, and plasma cutting.
ImportantSchool board and course safety information

This public website explains design and fabrication concepts. Machine-specific safety instruction, operating procedures, assessments, and authorization are intentionally kept outside the website. Follow your school board’s safety guidelines and the course-specific instruction provided by your teacher. Students in Mr. Andrade’s classes can find that information in Google Classroom. This page is not permission or authorization to operate equipment.

NoteWhy the name Systems Design & Fabrication?

Systems is broader than one material, machine, or course code. Designers decide how parts, materials, processes, fasteners, mechanisms, controls, people, and environments work together. Design & Fabrication keeps the pathway practical: important decisions become drawings, calculations, prototypes, parts, inspections, tests, and revisions.

Why this track

The Engineering Preparation Track develops mathematical modelling through electrical and control systems. This parallel reference develops physical design judgement through fabrication systems: understand a need, investigate precedents, communicate a proposal, select materials and processes, make and inspect parts, assemble the system, evaluate evidence, and improve the design.

The strongest Ontario course connections are TIJ/TAS, TDJ, TMJ, TCJ, TTJ, TEJ, TGJ, and THJ, but the technical knowledge is useful to anyone learning how designed objects are specified and made. Select the sections that match the problem, materials, available equipment, and required accuracy.

Every project returns to six questions:

  1. What is the situation, who is affected, and what is actually needed?
  2. What do existing products, users, standards, and tests teach us?
  3. What is the simplest prototype that can sell the idea clearly and produce useful feedback?
  4. What materials, mechanisms, fasteners, fits, and processes make the system work?
  5. What do measurements, tests, and synthesized feedback reveal about the proposal?
  6. Should we revise the solution, the problem statement, the stated needs, or all three?

Learning goals

Learning goal Why it matters
Use the N.I.C.E. Design Process to define a need, investigate precedents, create a minimal pitchable or viable product, pitch it, collect feedback, evaluate, and iterate. A project should begin with a situation and evidence, not a favourite machine.
Read and create sketches, orthographic views, section views, assembly drawings, dimensions, tolerances, and revision records. Other people must be able to interpret, make, inspect, assemble, and revise the work.
Use measurement, limits, fits, datums, and introductory GD&T to express function. Precision is useful only when it is connected to how a part must fit and work.
Explain loads, motion, interfaces, mechanisms, maintenance, and failure modes in a machine or product. A collection of parts becomes a system only when the relationships are understood.
Select materials, stock forms, fasteners, joining methods, and fabrication processes using stated criteria. The same geometry can succeed or fail depending on material and process.
Plan the order of cutting, shaping, drilling, threading, joining, finishing, and inspection. A good part made in the wrong order may be difficult to measure, assemble, or repair.
Compare manual processes with CNC, laser, additive, and other digital methods. Automation changes setup and repeatability, but does not remove design judgement.
Inspect a part, record deviations, test the assembly, synthesize feedback, and revise the editable source. The next version should contain what was learned from the physical result.

Technical foundations

0. N.I.C.E.: define, create, communicate, and evaluate

Begin with the complete N.I.C.E. Design Process.

  • Needs and necessities: describe the situation, the people affected, what must change, and the difference between a real need and a preferred feature.
  • Investigate and inquire: study users, precedents, failures, materials, mechanisms, standards, constraints, and unanswered questions.
  • Create and communicate: make the simplest version that can sell and test the proposal. Use a minimal pitchable product when the main goal is to make the idea understandable and persuasive, or a minimal viable product when a working function must be demonstrated. Minimal means the least complexity needed for the pitch or test, not a physically smaller object. Pitch the idea, ask focused questions, and collect feedback without defending the first version.
  • Evaluate: group and synthesize the feedback, compare it with the needs and criteria, re-question the situation and problem statement, decide whether the proposal meets the needs, and identify the next revision.

The cycle may return to any earlier stage. Evaluation can reveal that the design is weak, but it can also reveal that the original problem or assumed need was framed incorrectly.

1. Technical drawings, orthographic projection, and standards

A technical drawing is a controlled description of an object, not simply a picture. The drawing should communicate enough information for another person to manufacture and inspect the part without guessing.

Core drawing knowledge includes:

  • freehand design sketches, isometric or pictorial views, and multiview orthographic projection;
  • front, top, right-side, auxiliary, section, detail, assembly, and exploded views;
  • visible, hidden, centre, cutting-plane, section, dimension, extension, and leader lines;
  • title blocks, drawing numbers, part names, materials, units, scale, projection method, general tolerances, revisions, and approvals;
  • dimension placement that describes function without duplication, ambiguity, or hidden arithmetic;
  • part drawings for manufacturing and assembly drawings for relationships, item numbers, quantities, and orientation.

The drawing must identify the governing convention. Common references include the ASME Y14 family of product-definition standards and the ISO Geometrical Product Specifications system. Do not combine symbols or assumptions from different systems without making the chosen convention explicit.

2. Measurement, tolerances, GD&T, surface finish, and fits

Students should distinguish nominal size, actual size, limits, deviation, tolerance, allowance, accuracy, precision, and resolution. Use a rule, square, calliper, micrometer, height measurement, bore measurement, or fixed gauge according to the feature and required uncertainty.

Dimensioning and tolerance topics include:

  • unilateral, bilateral, limit, general, and reference dimensions;
  • datum features and datum reference frames;
  • size, location, orientation, form, profile, and runout requirements;
  • surface texture or finish where it affects sealing, friction, appearance, wear, or measurement;
  • tolerance stack-up across mating parts and assemblies;
  • inspection planning before fabrication begins.

Introductory GD&T should connect each control to function. Typical concepts include straightness, flatness, circularity, cylindricity, parallelism, perpendicularity, angularity, position, profile, circular runout, and total runout. ASME Y14.5 and ISO 1101 define formal systems; use the standard specified by the drawing or organization.

Fits describe the relationship between mating features:

Fit Relationship Typical design intent
Clearance fit the hole remains larger than the shaft across the specified limits free movement, sliding, assembly by hand
Transition fit the limits may produce a small clearance or a small interference accurate location with controlled assembly
Interference fit the shaft remains larger than the hole across the specified limits retained or torque-transmitting assembly

Also compare hole-basis and shaft-basis systems, running/sliding/location/press applications, temperature effects, finish, lubrication, and whether disassembly is required. The ISO 286 limits-and-fits system is one formal reference; classroom examples should focus first on functional relationships and measurable limits.

3. General machine and mechanism design

A machine changes force, motion, energy, material, or information. Begin with a system diagram showing inputs, outputs, boundaries, components, interfaces, and feedback.

Study these recurring design ideas:

  • force, torque, power, speed, work, energy, mechanical advantage, efficiency, and load paths;
  • tension, compression, shear, bending, torsion, bearing stress, deflection, wear, fatigue, impact, and buckling;
  • shafts, axles, bearings, bushings, keys, keyways, splines, pins, retaining rings, collars, and couplings;
  • gears, racks, lead screws, cams, linkages, pulleys, belts, sprockets, chains, springs, and linear guides;
  • fixed, rotating, sliding, pivoting, adjustable, detachable, and serviceable interfaces;
  • stiffness versus mass, alignment, balance, friction, lubrication, backlash, tolerance stack-up, and thermal expansion;
  • modularity, standard components, access for tools, replacement of wear parts, inspection, maintenance, and end-of-life disassembly.

A strong machine-design explanation follows the force and motion through the system and identifies where fit, alignment, fastening, or material choice controls performance.

4. Materials and process planning

Compare wood species and sheet goods, ferrous and non-ferrous metals, sheet thicknesses, engineering plastics, elastomers, composites, and additive-manufacturing materials. Consider function, stock form, strength, stiffness, toughness, hardness, machinability, weldability, grain direction, corrosion, heat, finish, cost, waste, reuse, repair, and availability.

A process plan identifies stock size, quantity, datums, setup order, workholding concept, tools, cutting or forming operations, joining sequence, intermediate inspections, finishing, and assembly. Explain why each operation occurs when it does.

5. Fasteners, threads, anchors, and assembly design

Choose a joint by asking whether it must be removable, adjustable, hidden, sealed, conductive, corrosion resistant, vibration resistant, permanent, or easy to inspect.

Threaded-fastener knowledge includes:

  • the difference between bolts, machine screws, cap screws, wood screws, sheet-metal screws, set screws, studs, and threaded rod;
  • metric diameter and pitch, inch diameter and threads per inch, coarse and fine series, right- and left-hand threads;
  • thread form, major/minor/pitch diameter, lead, engagement, tapped holes, clearance holes, and blind-hole depth;
  • nuts, plain washers, hardened washers, lock nuts, prevailing-torque features, thread-locking compounds, safety wire, cotter pins, and tab washers where applicable;
  • strength grade or property class, material, coating, corrosion compatibility, preload, joint stiffness, tightening method, and access for installation and service;
  • threaded inserts for wood, plastics, sheet metal, and damaged-thread repair;
  • rivets, rivet nuts, clinch fasteners, pins, dowels, keys, retaining rings, adhesives, and welds as alternatives or complements.

For fastening into concrete or masonry, distinguish concrete screws, wedge anchors, sleeve anchors, drop-in anchors, adhesive anchors, and systems intended for hollow masonry. Selection depends on base material, member thickness, edge distance, spacing, embedment, hole condition, tension and shear, environmental exposure, removability, and the manufacturer’s published design data. Permanent, structural, overhead, guard, handrail, or life-safety anchorage requires the applicable code, specified product system, and qualified design review rather than a generic classroom rule.

The assembly drawing should name each fastener, show quantity and location, state the hole or thread requirement, identify the stack of parts, and preserve tool access. A bill of materials should distinguish purchased hardware from fabricated parts.

6. Hand fabrication, hole features, threading, and finishing

Build accuracy through layout, sawing, filing, deburring, drilling, reaming, tapping, die threading, countersinking, counterboring, spotfacing, scraping, sanding, and finishing. Connect each hole feature to its function:

  • a clearance hole allows a fastener to pass;
  • a tap-drill hole leaves material for an internal thread;
  • a reamed hole controls size and finish for a pin, shaft, or accurate fit;
  • a counterbore recesses a cylindrical head or creates a flat seating surface;
  • a countersink seats a tapered head or removes a sharp hole edge;
  • a spotface creates a local flat bearing surface.

The evidence is a small assembly whose dimensions, hole features, threads, fasteners, finish, and service access match the drawing.

Material and forming processes

7. Wood preparation and shaping

Use the jointer to establish a reference face and edge, the planer to create consistent thickness, the band saw or another suitable process for shaping, and sanding equipment for controlled surface preparation. Track grain direction, moisture and stock defects, reference faces, initial and final dimensions, squareness, thickness, tear-out, and surface quality.

The result is stable, square stock for a later component or assembly. Explain where material was removed at each operation and how the reference surfaces control later measurements.

8. Sheet-metal systems

Develop flat patterns in paper or card before committing material. Study material thickness, inside bend radius, bend angle, neutral axis, bend allowance, bend deduction, K-factor as a model, reliefs, hems, seams, tabs, beads, flanges, and the effect of operation order.

Processes may include layout, shearing or sawing, notching, punching, drilling, deburring, bending, rolling, riveting, threaded inserts, screws, tabs, and resistance spot welding. Evidence should include the flat pattern, formed dimensions, bend sequence, joint plan, and fit with mating parts.

9. Metal cutting and stock preparation

Compare a horizontal band saw, vertical band saw configured for the material, powered hacksaw, cold saw, abrasive cut-off saw, manual hacksaw, plasma process, and CNC profile-cutting process. Select a method using stock shape, alloy, section thickness, required accuracy, heat input, kerf, burr, finish, speed, quantity, and later operations.

Stock preparation includes identifying the datum end, allowing material for cleanup, controlling cut sequence, deburring, marking part identity, and verifying the blank before machining or welding.

Welding processes

Welding processes are separated because their equipment, consumables, heat source, joint preparation, deposition, appearance, productivity, and suitable applications differ. For every process, record material, thickness, joint type, position, fit-up, machine settings, consumables, observations, distortion, and evaluation of the finished coupon.

10. Resistance spot welding

Resistance spot welding joins overlapping sheet by applying force and electrical resistance heating through electrodes. Study lap-joint geometry, electrode access, electrode face condition, sheet thickness, coating or surface condition, current, force, weld time, pitch, edge distance, indentation, nugget formation, and peel or destructive coupon evidence.

Use it when repeated sheet-metal lap joints and two-sided electrode access suit the design. It does not create a continuous sealed seam unless the selected process and joint are specifically designed to do so.

11. MIG / gas metal arc welding (GMAW)

GMAW continuously feeds wire through the welding gun. Study voltage, wire-feed speed, travel speed, contact-tip-to-work distance, torch angle, polarity, shielding gas, transfer mode, tack sequence, starts and stops, joint gap, bead profile, penetration evidence, spatter, undercut, porosity, overlap, burn-through, and distortion.

Compare butt, lap, tee, corner, fillet, and groove joints. Use labelled coupons to connect settings and technique to the resulting bead and joint rather than judging appearance alone.

12. Stick / shielded metal arc welding (SMAW)

SMAW uses a flux-coated consumable electrode. Study electrode classification, diameter, current range, polarity, arc length, work and travel angles, travel speed, bead placement, restarts, slag, multi-pass sequence, penetration evidence, inclusions, porosity, undercut, and distortion.

Compare how electrode choice, material thickness, joint preparation, position, and access affect the result. Keep the electrode classification and parameter record with each practice coupon.

13. TIG / gas tungsten arc welding (GTAW)

GTAW uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode and may use separate filler. Study current type and polarity, amperage control, tungsten selection and preparation, arc length, torch and filler angles, shielding gas, travel speed, filler timing, autogenous versus filler-added joints, cleanliness, heat input, bead profile, penetration evidence, oxidation, porosity, undercut, and distortion.

Compare the process where fine heat and filler control, thin material, clean appearance, or non-ferrous alloys matter. Document the relationship between joint fit-up, heat control, filler addition, and the finished coupon.

Machining and precision processes

14. Manual milling and common operations

Begin with machine axes, spindle direction, cutter types, workholding concepts, parallels, edge finding, datums, coordinates, stock allowance, speeds and feeds, depth and width of cut, conventional and climb cutting, and inspection between operations.

Common operations include:

  • squaring a block: create the first reference face, produce an adjacent square face, establish parallel/opposite faces, and bring the block to length, width, and thickness from controlled datums;
  • face milling and fly cutting for broad flat surfaces;
  • peripheral, side, and end milling for faces, shoulders, and profiles;
  • steps, open and closed slots, keyways, pockets, contours, and chamfers;
  • drilling, centre drilling or spotting, reaming, boring, countersinking, counterboring, spotfacing, and tapping;
  • circular features, bolt circles, angular setups, and simple indexing where equipment permits.

The setup sheet should identify stock, datums, coordinates, workholding concept, cutter sequence, target dimensions, tolerances, and inspection stages. A useful introductory project is a squared block or plate containing a step, slot, pocket, and inspected hole pattern.

15. Manual lathe and common operations

The lathe rotates the work around a centreline. Study workholding with chucks, collets, faceplates, or centres; tool height and geometry; stick-out; spindle speed; feed; depth of cut; tailstock use; centre drilling; and diameter/length inspection.

Common operations include:

  • facing to establish a reference end;
  • straight turning, step turning, shoulder turning, and finishing to diameter;
  • taper turning, form turning, radii, chamfers, and edge breaks;
  • centre drilling, drilling, boring, and reaming along the spindle axis;
  • grooving, undercutting, and parting;
  • external and internal threading by single-point tool, tap, die, or another suitable method;
  • knurling to create a grip or controlled surface pattern;
  • work between centres and simple eccentric work where the design and equipment justify it.

A turning plan should show the original stock, finished profile, centreline, datums, operation order, tools, dimensions, tolerances, surface requirements, and inspection points. Suitable parts include a spacer, bushing, pin, handle component, shaft feature, threaded adapter, or mating part for the milled component.

16. Surface grinding and precision finishing

Surface grinding controls flatness, parallelism, thickness, and finish on suitable work. Study wheel types and dressing concepts, work and chuck relationships, reference faces, downfeed and crossfeed as process variables, spark-out, heat effects, burrs, magnetic work suitability, and measurement of flatness, parallelism, thickness, and surface condition.

Evidence includes starting and final measurements, a drawing with functional tolerances, a process record, and an explanation of how heat, burrs, cleanliness, or reference choice affected the result.

Digital fabrication

17. CAD/CAM, CNC milling, routing, and engraving

CNC begins with the same drawing, datum, fit, workholding, and inspection discipline as manual machining. Define stock, fixtures, work coordinates, tool numbers, tool lengths, clearances, operations, stepdowns, stepovers, entry moves, tabs or holding strategy, and inspection points. Simulate the complete toolpath and preserve the editable CAD and CAM source.

  • CNC mill: accurate holes, pockets, slots, profiles, surfacing, engraving, and 2D/2.5D machined features.
  • CNC router: sheet goods, wood, plastics, fixtures, signs, furniture parts, and larger 2D/2.5D components.
  • CNC engraving: labels, scales, panels, serials, part identification, diagrams, and graphic surfaces.

Submit the source model, drawing, CAM setup, setup sheet, tool list, simulation evidence, machine file, first-part inspection, and revised source. Continue with CNC & Digital Machining.

18. Laser cutting, engraving, and 3D printing

Use Laser Cutting & 2D Fabrication for vector geometry, calibration, kerf, engraving, fit, assemblies, and machine-ready files. Use 3D Modelling + 3D Printing for parametric versus mesh modelling, source files, slicing, orientation, supports, tolerances, and revision.

A combined project can pair a calibrated laser-cut structure with a printed connector, spacer, jig, knob, cable guide, enclosure feature, or ergonomic interface. Justify why each process suits its part, then measure the mating interface and revise the source files.

19. Manual plasma cutting and CNC plasma concepts

Manual plasma cutting introduces torch-to-work relationship, piercing and edge starts, travel consistency, kerf, lead-in/lead-out choices, dross, heat-affected geometry, distortion, cut quality, and post-cut finishing.

CNC plasma adds CAD cleanup, closed profiles, nesting, cut order, lead geometry, consumable and kerf compensation concepts, torch height control, machine coordinates, CAM simulation, part identification, and first-part inspection. The Technology Commons CNC plasma system is still being built; this section describes the design and digital workflow without implying that the local machine is available.

Production and integration

20. Production systems, quality control, and integrated systems capstone

Move from one successful part to a repeatable process. Create a traveller or router, bill of materials, fixture or jig, setup record, inspection plan, sample frequency, nonconformance record, and revision. Compare cycle time, setup time, work in process, bottlenecks, rework, scrap, capability, and the value of interchangeable parts.

The integrated project should combine processes only when each one has a functional reason. Suitable systems include:

  • modular shop storage using prepared wood, sheet or machined brackets, laser/CNC parts, printed interfaces, and standard hardware;
  • an instrumented enclosure or test stand using sheet metal, machined features, engraving, fasteners, and TEJ controls;
  • a service cart or portable work module with a frame, replaceable panels, tool retention, and documented ergonomics;
  • a fixture, mechanism, machine accessory, display, or outdoor system that can be inspected, serviced, and improved.

Assessment model

Track work should reward design judgement and verified workmanship, not simply the number of machines used.

Category Suggested weight Evidence
Situation, needs, investigation, and alternatives 15% N.I.C.E. brief, precedents, questions, alternatives, and selection rationale
Drawings, standards, GD&T, fits, and process planning 20% views, dimensions, tolerances, interfaces, stock, sequence, and file control
Technical knowledge and design decisions 20% materials, mechanisms, fasteners, processes, calculations, and trade-offs
Fabrication and workmanship 20% suitable process selection, datums, fit, finish, assembly, and serviceability
Inspection, testing, feedback synthesis, and iteration 20% measurements, functional evidence, user feedback, deviations, failures, and revised source
Communication 5% organized portfolio, photographs, labels, references, pitch, and explanation

A polished object with no evidence should not receive a high result. Strong work makes the reasoning visible, records where the result differs from the drawing or need, and carries the evidence into the next version.

Technical standards references

Standards evolve and organizations may specify different systems. Always identify the standard, edition, units, projection method, and general drawing conventions used for a project.

  • ASME Y14 product-definition standards
  • ASME Y14.5: Dimensioning and Tolerancing
  • ISO 1101: Geometrical tolerancing
  • ISO limits and fits catalogue, including ISO 286
